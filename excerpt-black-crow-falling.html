Black Crow Falling
by Anton Jagelavicius
Overture

My eyes shot open to the blunt end of a baseball bat.

I blinked.

Beyond it, in the window—a woman in black silk pyjamas, two kids clinging to her legs. Not my brother’s wife. Not his kids.

“Get up!”

The voice jarred me and I shifted my gaze, following the bat up the arm of a bearded man in a red plaid shirt. I cleared my throat.

“I think I made a mistake.”

“Damn right you did. What the hell are you doing here?”

I thought about it for a moment. It wasn’t a terrible question. “I’m not sure, actually.” Then I forced a smile. “I was about to ask you the same thing.”

“Get out of my yard!” he shouted, jabbing me in the ribs like I was roadkill.

“I’m going. I’m going,” I said, looking up. My eyes rested on the face of his wife longer than I would have liked. The man knew who I was looking at. Of course he did.

“You need to leave. Now!” he shouted. Then the afterthought, “there are kids around!” I watched the two preschoolers jump and then hide behind their mom’s legs.

I couldn’t help but laugh at that. It was the worst kind of reflex—for anything remotely ironic, my face would immediately acquire the expression of someone mocking. It’s never served me well.

“What the hell are you laughing at?”

“I was just thinking…You could have told your kids you found the monster that was hiding under their bed. You could have been their hero forever!” I smiled.

His face contorted. “Get the fuck out of here. Move it.”

“That’s what I was hoping to do.”

He had a reason to be pissed, I suppose. All I had was the realization that my brother must have moved without telling. He didn’t need to, but it’s the least one expects. I tried sitting up, but it was almost pointless in my condition, having barely slept, strung out still on Amsterdam’s grass. I laughed awkwardly and steadied myself. It was like the rope mesh climb at the carnival; you think it’s a joke and wonder why so many people fall halfway up. So you try it out and discover some humility within you.

Falling is one way to find your Creator.

I reached over the edge of the hammock. Planted my hand into the muck.

For some reason I found that funny as well.

I looked over my shoulder to what lay beyond my torso—the other half of me, my arse in the air. I looked up at the lumberjack. “Maybe you can help me out?”

“No. Now. You’re leaving now!”

A smile on my face. The kind of smile that is irrational to those who claim to be reasonable.

The kids were still hiding behind their mom’s legs, and their momma’s face gave me no love—I was just disturbing her breakfast, and possibly responsible for a beautiful Saturday ruined.

I twisted the rest of my body out of the hammock, no longer caring if I was covered in mud. I ended up on all fours—like any other animal. Now the bat was hovering in front of my face and I had an idea: this scene rendered on a canvas, hanging in the National Gallery. Monster Found, mixed media, 2003. A monochromatic palette ranging from violet to plum, cut with slate grey—the colors of old bruises and a turbulent sky.

“Could I have a glass of water?” I asked. “I’m pretty thirsty after last night’s flight.” Incredulity on his face…a look that always gave me a certain pleasure to see, possibly because it’s rare and nobody is surprised by anything anymore.

The man looked at me funny, like I was kidding. Why would they think that?

“I’ll even hold the bat for you to watch over me while you’re gone.”

“Get the fuck out of here, idiot!”

If only he knew what the word really meant in Greek and that I was quite proud of occasionally being called an idiot.

I stood up and stretched, then the lumberjack stepped back, as if I smelled of piss.

That’s funny as well…how people will do things because they’ve been conditioned by their own perspective. Not funny. Maybe a bit ironic.

I noticed that his pretty wife was still staring at me—not in the way I would have liked, but in a way I needed, perhaps. A reminder that such women who cannot be ugly at 7 am are not really beautiful after all.

I slung the duffel bag over my shoulder and grabbed the handle of my beaten-up suitcase with its broken wheels. Hard to believe this shit piece of luggage carried about a quarter mil in uncut precious stones. I suppose that was also the point.

I thought I should tell them. Amuse myself as I watched their reaction.

Of course they’d never believe it. But assuming they did…they’d think up clever reasons to now welcome me inside, befriend me, invite me to barbecues with steaks instead of wieners. The lumberjack would invite me to the golf course on Sunday mornings. We’d watch blue herons fly over the ponds and sandtraps while shouting ‘four’, then split a bottle of Chablis on the 19th hole.

No thanks.

I dragged my case over the path. Flagstones—a grating sound and the realization that the broken wheel supports were scratching the limestone. I turned my head. Noticed the lumberjack wincing. The memory of my visit, now etched into his consciousness until moss grew over it.

I listened to the cedar gate snap shut behind me.

I was tempted to look back, curious if the kids had followed their mom into the living room to get a closer look at the man who’d been sleeping in their back yard.

I walked down the street. Five minutes separated me from the bus stop. There was no rush now. Spring had arrived.

The air was soft and humid. Birds were chirping. Leaves coming to life, fully aware they’d be dead in another five months so making the best of the time that they had. Absinthe, pistachio and sap green—colours that only nature can mix properly.

Good things to come. Still, I was cold after the long night. Barely slept. Then I thought of my parents in the city, wondering if I should’ve told them I was coming back, or whether it would be more fun to see the look on their faces when they answered the door.

I turned the corner onto the avenue and walked down the hill to the end of the street. Not much had changed in seven years: the bakery on the corner, the gas station across from it, and the bus shelter.

I went inside to see what had changed. Same aluminum bench, same schedule. But new graffiti on the plexiglass. I remembered initials in hearts; now it was Fuck you, a forest of nearly identical cocks drawn with a sharpie, and initial tags, left by kids who considered themselves artists because they were using one-point perspective.

Across the road, a girl was unpacking crates at a farmer’s kiosk. I had time before the bus came, so I crossed over. I expected the usual spring vegetables—fiddleheads, lettuce, asparagus—then a flash of red in the green crates appeared.

“Isn’t it early for strawberries?” I asked.

“First week of season.”

She had an accent, and dropped the definite article, which I thought was strange considering the French used it like butter.

“And you know,” she began to say, her voice rising, “it’s also the global warming.”

I nodded my head. She seemed pretty sure of it, frightened almost, like she’d just heard it on the news. News…funny. It’s been getting warmer since the last ice age.

“So you don’t need to grow them in greenhouses?”

“Quoi?”

“Serres chauffées.”

“Ah, ben oui. Of course.”

Of course. They were $3.50 a quart. Steep, but it had been awhile, so I dropped a fiver on the counter. She poured the strawberries into a green plastic bag—a color chosen to make you feel less guilty about the non-composting toxic plastic.

She picked up a calculator, an orange thing the size of a large paperback.

“It’s a buck-fifty change,” I said.

She ignored me—or was selectively deaf.

“A dollar fifty,” I repeated, slowly, in case she didn’t know what a “buck” was.

She glared and started over.

Math. I thought I was an imbecile because I couldn’t do differential equations. I inhaled deeply and smiled. “Incredible machine, isn’t it?”

“Hein?”

“That orange thing in your hand. Does it tell you the correct change?”

“C’est une calculatrice.” Again, the glare.

“Amazing.”

She pushed the coins toward me on the counter and quickly withdrew her hand. She probably thought I was one of those homeless people who asks for change at the lights. It’s true, my arms were laden with still-wet mud.

Back at the shelter, I sat on the bench and leaned against the plexiglass, tilting my head up toward the sun. I was greedy for the warmth, the way we are in the spring in Canada. Along the boulevard, a long line of sugar maples had burst into chartreuse while the spicy scent of pig shit drifted in from the farms. I inhaled deeply, thinking how if people didn’t know it was pig shit they might begin to appreciate it, rhapsodize about it like it was foie gras. It was an honest scent at least, and preferable to the synthesized and carcinogenic lavender spray people kept next to the toilet. I sat like that for several minutes, enjoying the quiet, the change of scenery, the soft breeze on my cheek, the sound of a bird in a tree calling out for a mate. I took a deep breath and closed my eyes. Sat still.

Thought of that monastery back in Thailand and how if I really wanted to, I could go there to leave the chaos, and never look back to think about what I’d be missing.

If I really wanted to do that.

It could be permanent.

Then the thought came to me; the idea that you can’t serve two masters. I hated clichés like this, mainly because they replaced thinking, but it felt real this time.

I took another long deep breath, thought how I no longer felt a heavy sense of loss, that I didn’t need to look back at what I’d missed or might be missing.

Then the cellphone in my pocket buzzed.

Seven thirty in the morning on a Saturday and someone was calling. I inhaled deeply and exhaled while thinking my breath had the power to make the sound go away. It kept buzzing. Then I remembered I still had my Spanish phone—early afternoon there. I counted seven pulses. Thought it might never end, so I pulled it out.

It was from Thailand—land of old enemies and a lost love. The thought of the latter made me pick up.

“Hello?” I answered.

“Max?”

I recognized the voice, but not the tone. It was Murph, and my body began to tense.

“Yeah?”

“I want you to listen closely. And don’t say a damn thing. Is that clear?”

I swallowed hard. “Uh huh.”

“Brett asked me to call you because he’s sick and tired of your shit.”

“What are you talking about?”

“Shut it, Max. Don’t play a fool. We know what you’ve been up to and this is our first and final warning. Just stop what you’re doing...”

I looked at the green bag. “I’m just eating strawberries...”

“I said stop it. Enough of the games. We know what you’ve been doing, and we know where you are.”

A pause. I waited for it.

“I still have my biker connections in Montreal, and believe me, you don’t want them knocking on your mom’s door. So stop everything. Now.”

Click. The line went dead.

My mouth went dry, the wind stopped, and the passing cars fell quiet. I pictured my parents’ house. Two bikers walking up the lawn, past the statue of Mary in the Grotto, knocking on the door, my mom answering, my dad close behind, grabbing his walking stick, ready to attack, like it was the war again.

And it was all because of me. More or less.

I did the math, and traced it back—who I’d spoken to, who might have tipped the first domino. I had a good idea of who it was, but it didn’t add up. None of it made much sense. All I’d done was go out of my way to help someone, and now that act had followed me here.

In the end, it’s never your sins they come for. It’s everything else.

Movement I
No Honour for the Trickster in His Hometown
2
Montreal, Spring 1996… Seven years earlier

I stood at the bottom of the escalator heading up to my first econometrics class. She was near the top when she turned and smiled, and remained like that—in a way that made me think I must know her from somewhere. I looked behind me. Nobody. I turned back to see her smile morphing into a gentle laugh.

Loose afro, high cheekbones, a slender face, guessing her ancestors descended from Ethiopia. I raised my hand—a half-wave, still uncertain. She nodded and waited for me at the top.

“Hi,” I said when I reached her.

“Hello...” she replied, still smiling. I trusted my gut and put aside my cynicism. Her name was Seta.

A few days later, we ran into each other in the library vestibule in the rush between classes. We stood there, smiling at each other, not moving, a stream of people brushing past us. The words came easily, though I don’t recall what they were. Don’t think she did either. It was the disappearance of time that disarmed us both.

“An hour passed? Really?” I asked.

She nodded rapidly and smiled. “Guess I missed my class.”

“I guess so.” I was about to ask her if it was worth it, when a faint voice in my head reminded me to keep my mouth shut.

We looked at each other like we’d just stepped off the same ledge. What’s next?

‘Next’ was her place. Almost every day, we’d work through econometrics problems that resembled haiku translated into Greek—three-line problems with not a number to be found: sigma, lambda, theta, beta, phi…and infinity. I’d be lost halfway through the first line and would have to go back to the beginning again, over and over…with no amount of coffee helping me to focus. My mind would wander into game theory, geometry, and other out-of-context ideas which were far more interesting. I’d occasionally pitch one of these ideas to Seta, like my method of finding the area of an ellipse using the Fibonacci sequence, and she’d listen closely, smile, and call me a genius.

But I would have been failing if it weren’t for her.

I’d often find myself watching her from across the coffee table, wondering if she could hear my thoughts, because there were often times she’d repeat whatever I was thinking. I’d often pick up the phone to call her and it wouldn’t ring because she’d just picked up to call me.

“Synchronicity,” she once told me.

I nodded my head. “You’re reading my mind.”

At the end of the semester, we were sitting on her fire escape smoking weed. She told me it was her first time, but she looked like a pro. Said she wanted to loosen up for the party we were hosting that night. Seta was taking slow drags, holding it in just long enough, letting the smoke drift out her nose, looking at me like there was nobody else she’d rather be with. I felt the same. We were talking about the possibility of parallel universes when she smiled curiously and lifted her eyes, like she’d received a mystical insight.
